Shipwrecked on Ancient Ground

The wreck of the Reindeer, Port Curtis, 1855

Scroll

A note before reading

Every line here comes from a cited record or from the book. Where the book tells the story, it says so. Where sources disagree, both are shown. Uncertain points are marked [CHECK]. The pictures are of the period and the places; none shows the Reindeer.

I

The Reef

Midnight, 26 September 1855

The person

Captain Edward Storey had raced another ship. The chart he trusted did not cover the corner he cut. (the book)

Admiralty chart 1749, Barrier Reefs from Raine Island to Cape York, surveyed by F.P. Blackwood 1843–44, published 1846. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

The document

Captain Edward Storey’s report, as summarised by Aberdeen Built Ships from the Sydney Empire, 18 January 1856

Reef, latitude 10°S, longitude 155°E.

At midnight on 26 September 1855 the forecastle lookout reported breakers on the lee bow.

The helm was put hard a-port. She would not weather it.

She struck, and filled to the level of the water outside.

The longboat was the only boat left.

They got her over the side with a little bread, 19 gallons of water, a set of instruments, and the ship’s papers.

Source

Admiralty chart 1749, Barrier Reefs from Raine Island to Cape York, surveyed by F.P. Blackwood 1843–44, published 1846. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

What the papers left out

The shortcut he took is not drawn on the report. This reading did not open the Empire page itself: the lines follow a summary, and they are a transcription, not a scan.

Admiralty chart 1749, Barrier Reefs from Raine Island to Cape York, surveyed by F.P. Blackwood 1843–44, published 1846. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

II

The Longboat

Bread, water, instruments, papers

The person

Men swear they hear mermaids; the sounds are dugongs. Flying fish are eaten raw. When food fails they drink the oil used for lamps. (the book)

Admiralty chart 2350, East Coast, Double Point to Cape Tribulation and the Barrier Reefs, from the HMS Rattlesnake survey 1843–48, published 1855. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

What the papers left out

A later biography agrees about the longboat, the fear along the coast, and Port Curtis. It does not agree about the dates. The report lists what went into the boat; it does not list who.

Admiralty chart 2350, East Coast, Double Point to Cape Tribulation and the Barrier Reefs, from the HMS Rattlesnake survey 1843–48, published 1855. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

III

Port Curtis

Ancient ground

The chart

Cook and Flinders had mapped much of the east coast while their ships kept moving. This chart is Flinders’ sheet for this stretch of coast, with later additions. The book says they did not map the shortcut that wrecked her.

Chart of Terra Australis, Sheet III, East Coast. Surveyed by Matthew Flinders 1799–1803, with additions by P.P. King (1826) and F.P. Blackwood (1844). Published 1814. Admiralty (Capt. Hurd, Hydrographer). Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

The harbour

A ship had entered Port Curtis more than fifty years before the longboat.

Lady Nelson, which the State Library of Queensland’s notes call the first ship to enter Port Curtis Harbour, 6 August 1802. Artist unknown; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

The ground

The longboat was never the beginning of this ground. The land already had its own people and its own boundaries. The app names Bailai, Gurang, Gooreng Gooreng, Taribelang Bunda, Gangulu and Iman / Yiman, and does not speak for any of them.

Gladstone seen from a hill, about 1870, roughly fifteen years after the longboat came in. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

The document

The manuscript’s record note: most of the crew went to Sydney in the Sabio Chief in early January 1856. Joseph Bennedick stayed. Walter David Taylor Powell joined the Native Police in December 1855, weeks after the longboat came in.

What the papers left out

No surviving crew list is printed in this reading. The papers never print the names of the people who watched the beach.

Gladstone seen from a hill, about 1870, roughly fifteen years after the longboat came in. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

IV

The Papers

Young’s station, Christmas 1855

The document

Weeks later, newspapers reported an attack on Mr Young’s station near Port Curtis at the end of December 1855.

The Empire, 28 January 1856, as quoted by the same map (the ellipses are theirs)

“On the 27th December, a large party of these rascals made a rush upon Mr Young’s station,

and killed a white man … two others …

A white man and his wife … and two civilized blacks…”

Source

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

Sydney Morning Herald, 18 February 1856, p. 5, as quoted by the University of Newcastle colonial frontier massacres map

“the blacks came and murdered every person on the establishment —

three men and two women, —

besides some other blacks which Mr. Young had on the place.”

Source

The insulting words are the papers’. They are printed so you can see the papers at work, not so you will copy them.

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

What the papers left out

The two papers do not agree on the date or the count. The book says the twenty-sixth, and five people. Neither paper describes the weeks before. The rides that followed would be called “dispersal”: that is the officials’ word, and the clippings do not count the camps.

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain.

The pages remain open.
