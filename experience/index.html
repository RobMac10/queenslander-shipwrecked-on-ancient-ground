Queenslander: Reopen the Records

Shipwrecked on Ancient Ground

The wreck of the Reindeer, Port Curtis, 1855: a document trail

Scroll

The Ninth Wave, 1850. Survivors cling to a mast after a wreck. Not the Reindeer: a painting from the same decade. Ivan Aivazovsky. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

How to read this

Each scene starts with a document: a report, a newspaper, a chart, a biography. Then comes what that document leaves out. Where records disagree, both are shown. Uncertain points are marked [CHECK]. No picture shows the Reindeer or her crew. The sources contain the deaths of Aboriginal people.

I

The Reef

September 1855

Document 1 · the captain’s report

Captain Edward Storey’s report, as summarised by Aberdeen Built Ships from the Sydney Empire, 18 January 1856

Reef, latitude 10°S, longitude 155°E.

At midnight on 26 September 1855 the forecastle lookout reported breakers on the lee bow.

The helm was put hard a-port. She would not weather it.

She struck, and filled to the level of the water outside.

The longboat was the only boat left.

They got her over the side with a little bread, 19 gallons of water, a set of instruments, and the ship’s papers.

Source

Admiralty chart 1749, Barrier Reefs from Raine Island to Cape York, surveyed by F.P. Blackwood 1843–44, published 1846. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 2 · the survivor’s account, seventy years on

Morning Bulletin (Rockhampton), 25 August 1924, “Sixty Years in Queensland: Captain Powell’s Wonderful Career”, from Powell’s own account taken down about 1900

“The Reindeer was engaged in the China tea trade, and she left Melbourne on September 6, 1855, for Shanghai.”

“The ship Chrysolite left Melbourne for Shanghai on the same day, and the two captains decided to test their ships…”

“…till September 25, when she ran on the Pocklington Reef, among the Louisiade Archipelago.”

“There were nineteen men on board the Reindeer.”

Trove

Admiralty chart 1749, Barrier Reefs from Raine Island to Cape York, surveyed by F.P. Blackwood 1843–44, published 1846. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

What the record leaves out

The two accounts disagree. Storey gives midnight on 26 September and 10°S 155°E. Powell’s account gives 25 September and Pocklington Reef. A later biography (Wikipedia) gives 5 September, and has her leaving Melbourne in August, not on 6 September (the digit is unclear in the scanned 1924 text) [CHECK]. None of them names the other seventeen men.

Admiralty chart 1749, Barrier Reefs from Raine Island to Cape York, surveyed by F.P. Blackwood 1843–44, published 1846. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

II

The Longboat

Nineteen men, one boat

Document 3 · the coast

Morning Bulletin (Rockhampton), 25 August 1924, “Sixty Years in Queensland: Captain Powell’s Wonderful Career”, from Powell’s own account taken down about 1900

“A landing was made on the mainland near Cape Grafton…”

“Two of the crew said they would remain ashore…”

“A few shots were fired…”

“Their misery was increased by several fights … both on the coastal islands and on the mainland.”

“There was some sweet oil on board … When that was all done … colza oil, of which they had one tin.”

Trove

The papers’ words for Aboriginal people are insulting. They are printed here as evidence of how the papers wrote, not for re-use.

Admiralty chart 2350, East Coast, Double Point to Cape Tribulation and the Barrier Reefs (HMS Rattlesnake survey 1843–48), published 1855. Cape Grafton lies on this sheet. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

What the record leaves out

The people at Cape Grafton and along the coast are seen only from the boat. Their names, their reasons and their losses are not in this account, and no account of theirs was printed.

Admiralty chart 2350, East Coast, Double Point to Cape Tribulation and the Barrier Reefs (HMS Rattlesnake survey 1843–48), published 1855. Cape Grafton lies on this sheet. United Kingdom Hydrographic Office. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

III

Port Curtis

November 1855

Document 4 · arrival

Morning Bulletin (Rockhampton), 25 August 1924, “Sixty Years in Queensland: Captain Powell’s Wonderful Career”, from Powell’s own account taken down about 1900

“Continuing south they found the passage between Curtis and Facing Islands, and so went through to the Gladstone Harbour…”

“…the Government boat was sent out to meet the exhausted men.”

“Lieutenant John Murray … in command of the Native Police Force for the Gladstone district, was in need of officers…”

“…sworn in … ten days after his arrival, half dead from starvation.”

Trove

Chart of Terra Australis, Sheet III, East Coast, surveyed by Matthew Flinders 1799–1803, with additions to 1844. Published 1814. Admiralty (Capt. Hurd, Hydrographer). Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 5 · a date in dispute

Powell’s 1924 account dates the arrival in “November” (the day is unclear in the scanned text) [CHECK]. Wikipedia’s biography gives 25 November 1855 and has Powell enlisting in December 1855.

Lady Nelson, which the State Library of Queensland’s notes call the first ship to enter Port Curtis Harbour, 6 August 1802. Artist unknown; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Whose harbour

The Gladstone region is Bailai, Gurang, Gooreng Gooreng and Taribelang Bunda Country, as recognised in a native title determination (Wikipedia: Gladstone).

What the record leaves out

No surviving crew list is printed in these sources. The 1924 account names Powell and one shipmate. It does not name Joseph Bennedick.

Gladstone seen from a hill, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

IV

Jos

Joseph Bennedick of Calliope

Document 6 · his marriage and his life

Bundaberg Mail, 17 September 1915, “Passing of a Pioneer” (from the Gladstone Observer)

“Miss Mary Ann Kerr (her maiden name) was married in the same year [1863] to Mr Joseph Bennedick, the ceremony taking place at Rockhampton…”

“…at Calliope Mr and Mrs Bennedick spent their lives with the exception of a short residence in Sydney.”

“Mr Bennedick passed away three years ago, having exceeded the allotted span of three score years and ten.”

Trove

Drovers and farm hands in the drafting yards, Jimbour Station, Queensland, 1869. Pastoral work of the period; not Calliope, and no one here is identified as Joseph Bennedick. State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 7 · where he was born

Central Queensland Herald, 3 October 1946, obituary of his son, Joseph Bennedick sen. of Calliope, born 1868

“The father of the deceased was born in Austria, and his mother in Ireland.”

“The late Mr. Bennedick had done a great deal of station work, and made many droving trips…”

Trove

Drovers and farm hands in the drafting yards, Jimbour Station, Queensland, 1869. Pastoral work of the period; not Calliope, and no one here is identified as Joseph Bennedick. State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 8 · the goldfield

Gold was found at Calliope in 1862, and the next year it became Queensland’s first proclaimed goldfield (Wikipedia: Calliope).

Daily Northern Argus, 22 September 1888, Supreme Court, Rockhampton

“Joseph Benedick deposed: I am a miner residing at Calliope…”

Trove

This may be Jos or his son Joseph (born 1868, who would then be about 20). [CHECK: elder or son]

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 9 · sheep and gold, later

Later Gladstone-district papers

1896: “Bennedick Bros. are now sending away a small parcel” of ore, Gladstone mining news.

1901: “…Joseph Bennedick in charge: 12 000 sheep are passing, travelling to truck…” for the Gladstone Meat Works.

1904: “the Seaview claim crushing (Bennedick Bros.)” at Tableland.

1896 (Trove)

1901 (Trove) · 1904 (Trove)

By these dates Jos’s sons were grown men, so “Joseph Bennedick” and “Bennedick Bros.” may be the next generation. [CHECK]

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

What the record leaves out

No document found here puts Joseph Bennedick in the Reindeer’s longboat [CHECK: source needed]. No record has yet been found of him shepherding, or at Canoona in 1858 [CHECK: source needed]. What can be shown: a man born in Austria, married at Rockhampton in 1863, who lived at Calliope until about 1912.

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

V

Powell and the Native Police

1855–1863

Document 10 · the commission

Morning Bulletin (Rockhampton), 25 August 1924, “Sixty Years in Queensland: Captain Powell’s Wonderful Career”, from Powell’s own account taken down about 1900

June 1856: Powell escorts Richard Palmer to the Fitzroy; “his troopers … built what was the first dwelling-house in Rockhampton.”

Later the Archers “invited Powell to change his camp to Gracemere.”

Promoted First Lieutenant; “For a short period he was stationed at … near Hornetbank, and was largely concerned in punishing the murderers of the Fraser family.”

Trove

Inspector John Murray, Native Police, about 1866. Murray commanded the Native Police at Port Curtis in 1855 and swore Powell in. State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 11 · the force

The Native Police were Aboriginal troopers under white officers. At Rannes on 23 September 1855, days before the Reindeer struck, the Native Police barracks was attacked and three troopers were killed (Wikipedia: Rannes).

Wikipedia, “Walter David Taylor Powell” (citing Gordon Reid, A Nest of Hornets, and the 1861 Select Committee)

1857: Lieutenant, Eurombah barracks on the Dawson River.

After Hornet Bank: troopers and armed settlers kill five, then nine more; raids at Taroom and Juandah follow.

1860–61: an arrested Aboriginal trooper is shot dead by Powell “in order to prevent his escape”.

1861: the Select Committee on the Native Police examines the killings; Powell is not censured.

Wikipedia

A Native Mounted Police detachment with its officers, Rockhampton, 1 December 1864. The troopers’ names are recorded on Commons; they are not repeated here. Unknown photographer. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

What the record leaves out

The accounts use words like “punishing” and “in order to prevent his escape”. These are the officials’ words, quoted, not descriptions. The troopers were Aboriginal men, but their own accounts are missing, and so are those of the people they rode against. No record found here places Joseph Bennedick with the force.

Herbert River Native Mounted Police camp, about 1870. A camp of the same force, further north; not Powell’s. Unknown photographer. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

VI

The Papers

Young’s station, December 1855

Document 12

The Empire, 28 January 1856, as quoted by the same map (the ellipses are theirs)

“On the 27th December, a large party of these rascals made a rush upon Mr Young’s station,

and killed a white man … two others …

A white man and his wife … and two civilized blacks…”

Trove

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Document 13

Sydney Morning Herald, 18 February 1856, p. 5, as quoted by the University of Newcastle colonial frontier massacres map

“the blacks came and murdered every person on the establishment —

three men and two women, —

besides some other blacks which Mr. Young had on the place.”

Trove

The papers’ words for Aboriginal people are insulting. They are printed here as evidence of how the papers wrote, not for re-use.

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

What the record leaves out

The two papers disagree on the day and on the count. Neither says what happened on the station in the weeks before, and neither counts the camps that were attacked afterwards.

Auckland Creek, Gladstone, about 1870. Richard Daintree; State Library of Queensland. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

VII

Friction hot spots

Frontier conflict around Port Curtis, the Fitzroy and the Dawson, 1855–1888. The numbers on the map match the list. Positions marked [CHECK] are approximate.

  1. Port Curtis / Gladstone · 25 Nov 1855 (biography) [CHECK]
    The Reindeer’s longboat reaches the harbour; Lieutenant John Murray of the Native Police is in command for the district. Bailai, Gurang, Gooreng Gooreng and Taribelang Bunda Country (native title determination).
    Morning Bulletin, 25 Aug 1924 · Wikipedia: Gladstone
  2. Rannes · 23 Sep 1855
    Native Police barracks attacked at night; three troopers killed. Murray’s force pursues.
    Wikipedia: Rannes · Empire, 22 Apr 1856
  3. Young’s station, Mount Larcombe [CHECK] · end Dec 1855
    Attack on the station; the papers disagree on the day and the count.
    Empire, 28 Jan 1856 · SMH, 18 Feb 1856 · Newcastle massacres map
  4. Nankin Creek [CHECK] · Jan 1856
    A massacre of local Aboriginal people by Lieutenant John Murray’s Native Police, as Wikipedia records it.
    Wikipedia: Canoona
  5. Rockhampton · June 1856
    Sub-Lieutenant Powell escorts Richard Palmer to the Fitzroy; his troopers build the first dwelling, a barracks. Darumbal Country.
    Morning Bulletin, 25 Aug 1924 · Wikipedia: Rockhampton
  6. Gracemere · 1856
    At the Archers’ request Powell moves his troopers’ camp to Gracemere station.
    Morning Bulletin, 25 Aug 1924
  7. Canoona [CHECK] · late 1858
    Queensland’s first gold rush; about 15,000 people arrive in a few months.
    Wikipedia: Australian gold rushes
  8. Hornet Bank, upper Dawson [CHECK] · 27 Oct 1857
    Eleven settlers killed by a mostly Yiman group. Powell, now at Eurombah, leads reprisals; an estimated 150 Aboriginal people are killed in the expeditions that follow.
    Wikipedia: Hornet Bank massacre · Wikipedia: W.D.T. Powell
  9. Taroom and Juandah [CHECK] · Dec 1857
    Raids led by Powell with settlers; Wikipedia, citing Gordon Reid, records people shot at Taroom and eleven at Juandah.
    Wikipedia: W.D.T. Powell
  10. Dee River crossing [CHECK] · 1860–61
    Powell shoots an arrested Aboriginal trooper dead “in order to prevent his escape”, the official phrase.
    Wikipedia: W.D.T. Powell · Moreton Bay Courier, 24 Jan 1861 (cited there)
  11. Calliope goldfield · 1862–63; 1888
    Gold found 1862; Queensland’s first proclaimed goldfield 1863. A Joseph Benedick, “miner residing at Calliope”, gives evidence in 1888 [CHECK: elder or son].
    Wikipedia: Calliope · Daily Northern Argus, 22 Sep 1888
